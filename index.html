When geography hinders growth

On 6 August, we mark the International Day of Awareness of the Special Development Needs and Challenges of Landlocked Developing Countries. Home to over 600 million people, the 32 LLDCs often face high trade costs, limited diversification, and infrastructure shortfalls that impact growth. Through the Awaza Programme of Action (2024–2034), the UN seeks to ensure trade better facilitation, structural transformation, transport connectivity, technology, climate resilience, and support. Its goal is to align national-level actions, regional integration, and policy frameworks to put LLDCs on a resilient path to sustainable development.

OUTER SPACE, UNEP

Shoot for the Moon: Can space exploration be sustainable?

The Moon has fascinated humanity for millennia and plays a vital role in sustaining life on Earth through its influence on tides and ecosystems. As lunar exploration accelerates, with countries and private companies pursuing ambitious missions, the United Nations is emphasizing the need for sustainable space activities. Despite international treaties and guidelines, an estimated 140 million pieces of space debris orbit Earth, including defunct satellites, rocket bodies, collision fragments and mission-related debris. These objects can damage spacecraft, pollute the atmosphere during re-entry and even pose risks to people and ecosystems. The United Nations Environment Programme and the UN Office for Outer Space Affairs are working to address these growing environmental challenges through stronger global cooperation and governance.

HUMAN RIGHTS, OHCHR

Sudan's gum arabic trade fuels war economy

A resin that helps bind everything from soft drinks to medicines now at the centre of Sudan's devastating war economy, according to a new UN Human Rights report. Sudan's gum arabic trade, long vital to global food, pharmaceutical and cosmetics industries, has become deeply entangled in the country's conflict. Sudan, which once supplied 70-80% of global crude gum arabic exports, now sees the commodity linked to conflict financing, with armed actors benefiting through territorial control, checkpoints, taxation and smuggling. Farmers and traders face insecurity, restricted movement and disrupted livelihoods, while human rights abuses have been documented in producing areas. The report urges stronger oversight, anti-smuggling measures and heightened human rights due diligence across the supply chain.

AGRICULTURE AND FOOD, FAO

A tiny fly, a growing threat across the Americas

The New World Screwworm (NWS) is not a worm but a parasitic fly whose larvae burrow into living tissue, causing painful and potentially fatal infestations in livestock, wildlife, pets and, occasionally, humans. Resurging across the Americas, it threatens animal health, food production, trade and rural livelihoods. NWS spreads through the movement of infested animals, migrating wildlife and adult flies crossing borders, making surveillance and international cooperation essential. A proven solution is the Sterile Insect Technique, which has successfully eradicated the pest in several countries. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), together with partners, supports countries through surveillance, veterinary training, early warning systems, emergency assistance and regional coordination to prevent, control and ultimately eradicate NWS.

Maintain International Peace and Security

Structure of the United Nations

The main parts of the UN structure are the General Assembly, the Security Council, the Economic and Social Council, the Trusteeship Council, the International Court of Justice, and the UN Secretariat. All were established in 1945 when the UN was founded.

General Assembly

The General Assembly is the main deliberative, policymaking and representative organ of the UN. All 193 Member States of the UN are represented in the General Assembly, making it the only UN body with universal representation.

Security Council

The Security Council has primary responsibility, under the UN Charter, for the maintenance of international peace and security. It has 15 Members (5 permanent and 10 non-permanent members). Each Member has one vote. Under the Charter, all Member States are obliged to comply with Council decisions.

Economic and Social Council

The Economic and Social Council is the principal body for coordination, policy review, policy dialogue and recommendations on economic, social and environmental issues, as well as implementation of internationally agreed development goals.

Trusteeship Council

The Trusteeship Council was established in 1945 by the UN Charter, under Chapter XIII, to provide international supervision for 11 Trust Territories that had been placed under the administration of seven Member States, and ensure that adequate steps were taken to prepare the Territories for self-government and independence.

International Court of Justice

The International Court of Justice is the principal judicial organ of the United Nations. Its seat is at the Peace Palace in the Hague (Netherlands). It is the only one of the six principal organs of the United Nations not located in New York (United States of America).

Secretariat

The Secretariat comprises the Secretary-General and tens of thousands of international UN staff members who carry out the day-to-day work of the UN as mandated by the General Assembly and the Organization's other principal organs.

Seven strategies to prevent drowning

Drowning claims more than 300,000 lives each year, yet most deaths are preventable. Many countries still lack the policies, laws, and governance needed to reduce water-related risks.

World Health Organization's guidance outlines seven evidence-based strategies, including safer infrastructure, swimming education, rescue systems, and flood preparedness. Together, these measures help governments strengthen drowning prevention while advancing public health, safety, and equity.

Use AI responsibly for a better world (AAN: S13-E7)

"I would not trust AI. You are not supposed to trust AI. You are supposed to use it, always doubt it, always verify what it tells you, cross-validate, so that you can be able to use it responsibly."

Growing up in rural South Africa, Tshilidzi Marwala first learned about engineering from his grandmother's handmade clay pots. Now rector of the United Nations University, the Under-Secretary-General is a vocal advocate of using artificial intelligence (AI) for good.

Education has been a driving force throughout Tshilidzi Marwala's life, alongside the seismic transitions he has witnessed in his native country and around the world. "We [returned] to South Africa just after the end of apartheid and then we realized that freedom is beautiful... Democracy is a beautiful concept, but it's a very difficult journey. It's not an overnight thing."

With a doctorate in neural networks among his many academic achievements, the UNU rector is familiar with the architecture behind the current rapid developments in AI. In this episode, he reflects on the intersection of political freedom, education and technology, and shares why digital literacy is among the most important skills of our age.

Venezuela: Where hope still stands

One month after Venezuela's devastating twin earthquakes, hope endures despite overwhelming loss. More than 5,300 people have died, 16,740 have been injured and nearly 18,000 people have lost their homes across seven affected states. International search-and-rescue teams from 31 countries joined local responders, while humanitarian organizations continue to provide food, health care, shelter, clean water, protection and psychosocial support to thousands of people. As communities begin the long road to recovery, resilience, solidarity and sustained humanitarian assistance remain essential to help families rebuild their lives.

25 new sites inscribed on the World Heritage List

The UNESCO World Heritage Committee meets annually to decide which cultural and natural sites deserve World Heritage status and to monitor the conservation of existing sites. At its 48th session in Busan, Republic of Korea, the Committee inscribed 25 new sites on the World Heritage List, including Wadi Wurayah (pictured), recognized for its ecological distinctiveness and biodiversity. The Committee adopted measures to strengthen the protection of heritage threatened by conflict and climate change, and reinforced efforts to make the List more representative, reaffirming a shared global commitment to safeguarding humanity's cultural and natural heritage for future generations.
